Ask anyone what SOS stands for and you will get "Save Our Souls", or "Save Our
Ship", or occasionally "Send Out Succour". All three are inventions. The signal
came first and the phrases were fitted to it afterwards.

## Why this pattern

The delegates at the 1906 International Radiotelegraphic Convention in Berlin
needed a distress call that could not be confused with anything else. Radio in
1906 was noisy, operators were inexperienced, and a call for help that could be
misheard as ordinary traffic was worse than useless.

So they picked on sound, not meaning:

```
...---...
```

Three short, three long, three short. Nine elements in a strict symmetrical
shape, with nothing else in the code that resembles it. Through interference,
at the edge of readability, half-heard — it is still recognisable. That is the
entire reason it was chosen.

It came into force on 1 July 1908.

## It is one character, not three

This is the part that gets lost. SOS is a **prosign**: it is sent as a single
continuous signal, with no gaps between what look like letters.

Written properly it has a bar over it — S̅O̅S̅ — which is the convention for "run
these together as one character".

That matters, because if you send it as three separate letters:

```
...   ---   ...
```

with proper three-unit gaps, you have sent the letters S, O and S. It is not
wrong exactly, but it is not the distress signal, which is a nine-element shape
with one-unit gaps throughout. Run together it lasts about two seconds at
15 WPM and has a completely different rhythm.

The same is true of the other prosigns you will meet. `-.-.-` is the "starting
transmission" signal, not the letters K and A.

## What it replaced

Before 1906 the Marconi company used **CQD** — `-.-. --.- -..` — which is
sometimes explained as "Come Quick, Danger". That is also a backronym. CQ was
(and remains) the general call to any station, from the French _sécurité_; the
D was simply appended for distress.

Both were in use for a while. The _Titanic_ sent both in 1912, which is often
retold as the moment SOS took over, though it had already been the standard for
four years by then.

## Why "Save Our Souls" stuck

Because it is useful. Nine elements are hard to remember; a phrase is easy. The
backronym is a mnemonic that escaped and became a fact, which happens to a lot
of things that are genuinely just arbitrary.

It is worth knowing it is a mnemonic, though, because the "it stands for
something" framing leads people to send it as three letters — and the whole
point of the signal is the shape.

## Hear it

You can [play SOS](/words/sos/) at any speed, or key it yourself on the
[telegraph](/telegraph/) and see whether your gaps hold together. Sending it as
one unbroken character is harder than it looks, which is rather the point.

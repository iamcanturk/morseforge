Almost everyone starts the same way. You find a table of the alphabet, you print
it, and you set about memorising twenty-six patterns of dots and dashes. Within
a week you can recite them. Within a month you have hit a wall you cannot
explain, somewhere around eight or ten words per minute, and no amount of
practice moves it.

The chart did that.

## What the chart actually teaches

When you learn `A = .-` from a table, you are learning a _visual_ fact. So when
you later hear a short beep followed by a long one, your brain does this:

1. Hear the sounds
2. Convert them to dots and dashes
3. Recall which letter has that pattern
4. Say "A"

A fluent operator does one step: hear it, know it. The same way you do not spell
out a written word letter by letter before recognising it.

Those extra steps cost time, and the cost is fixed. At 5 words per minute there
is room for them. At 15 there is not, and the whole system collapses — which is
why the wall arrives suddenly rather than gradually.

## Why the wall is at 10 WPM specifically

At 10 words per minute a dot lasts 120 milliseconds and a whole character takes
under half a second. Conscious recall runs at roughly half a second per item.
You are not slow because you lack practice; you are slow because you are asking
a deliberate process to keep up with an automatic one.

Speeding up the conscious step does not work. The step has to go.

## What to do instead

**Learn each character as a sound from the first minute.** Not as dots and
dashes you then convert — as a rhythm you recognise directly, the way you
recognise a doorbell without decomposing it.

**Use Farnsworth timing.** Send each character at a fast speed, but leave long
gaps between characters. This is the single most useful thing a beginner can
change. Slowing the characters themselves is what invites counting; keeping them
fast makes counting impossible, and the long gaps give you time to recall
without it.

If you are practising at 5 words per minute overall, send the characters at 18
and stretch the gaps. It feels harder for about two days and then it does not.

**Send as well as listen.** Keying a character is a motor memory, and motor
memory attaches to the rhythm rather than to the written shape. It is also the
fastest way to find out that your idea of a dash is too short.

## Is the chart useless?

No — it is useful for exactly two things: looking up a character you have
forgotten, and understanding _why_ the codes are what they are. The tree
structure behind Morse is genuinely interesting, and knowing that E is one dot
because E is the most common letter in English makes the whole alphabet feel
designed rather than arbitrary.

Just do not try to memorise it. Read it once, understand the shape of it, then
close it and go and listen to something.

## Where to start

The [seven-day plan](/learn-morse-code-in-7-days/) teaches the alphabet in the
order the code is built, one layer of the tree per day, with audio from the
first character. It takes about two hours in total.

If you would rather start by sending than by listening, the
[telegraph](/telegraph/) turns your keyboard into a straight key — and the
timing there is deliberately forgiving, so hesitating mid-character does not
punish you.
